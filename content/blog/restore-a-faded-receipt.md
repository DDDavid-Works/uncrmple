You need a receipt for an expense claim, a return or your tax records, and when you finally dig it out of your wallet the print has turned pale grey. The total is still there if you squint, but a photo of it looks like a blank strip of paper.

**The short answer:** photograph the receipt in bright daylight without the flash, then straighten the photo, even out the lighting and raise the contrast. That brings back faint print as long as some of it is still on the paper. Print that has faded away completely cannot be restored by any app.

A faded receipt is often more readable than it looks. How much you can recover depends on how much print is left, so it helps to know what is happening to the paper.

## Why thermal paper receipts fade

Most shop and till receipts are printed on thermal paper. There is no ink: the paper is coated with a chemical layer that turns dark where the printer heats it. That is why receipt printers never need ink cartridges, and also why the print does not last.

The dark marks slowly fade back, and some things speed this up:

- **Heat**, such as a car dashboard, a sunny windowsill or a pocket close to your body.
- **Light**, especially direct sunlight.
- **Friction and folding**, which rub the coating off along creases.
- **Oils and chemicals** from hands, hand cream, hand sanitiser and some plastic sleeves.

A receipt kept flat in a dark drawer can stay readable for years. One kept crumpled in a wallet can be pale within months.

## Can a faded receipt be restored?

Often, yes. Faded print is low contrast: the marks are still darker than the paper, but only slightly. A camera usually records more of that difference than your eye picks out, especially in good light. Restoring the photo means stretching that small difference back into clear dark text on white paper.

That works as long as the marks are still there, even faintly. If the coating has faded back completely, or rubbed off along a fold, there is nothing left in the photo to bring back. No photo editor can recover it honestly, and a tool that "fills in" missing digits is guessing. On a receipt, a guessed amount or date is worse than a gap, so treat any result that looks too perfect with suspicion.

## How to photograph a faded receipt

The photo matters more for a faded receipt than for any other document, because there is so little contrast to work with.

- **Use bright, even daylight.** More light means the camera records more of the faint marks and less noise.
- **Turn off the flash.** Thermal paper is slightly glossy, and a flash leaves a bright patch that hides the print underneath.
- **Flatten it.** Press out the curl under a book for a minute, or hold the ends down. Creases cast small shadows that break up the lines of text.
- **Use a dark background.** A dark table makes the edges of the pale paper easy to find.
- **Fill the frame**, with the whole receipt in the photo and a little background around it. Long receipts can be photographed in two halves if the text gets too small.
- **Keep it sharp.** Tap to focus on the text and hold still. Faint print and blur together leave very little to work with.

## How to make a faded receipt photo readable

Once you have the photo, restoring it has three steps:

1. **Straighten it.** Find the edges of the receipt, correct the angle and crop away the background, so the receipt reads like a scan. [How to straighten a photo of a document taken at an angle](/blog/straighten-a-document-photo/) explains how this works.
2. **Even out the lighting.** Shadows and uneven light make a pale receipt look patchy. Removing them first means the faint print is treated the same everywhere on the paper. See [how to remove shadows from a photo of a document](/blog/remove-shadows-from-a-document-photo/).
3. **Increase the contrast.** Darken the faint marks and whiten the paper. A grayscale result usually keeps the most of very faint print. Black and white looks cleaner and prints well, but it turns every pixel either black or white, so the faintest strokes can drop out. Compare the two before you keep one.

## How to keep receipts from fading

The best fix for a faded receipt is to photograph it before it fades.

- **Photograph receipts on the day you get them**, especially ones you will need for expenses, warranties or taxes.
- **Store paper receipts flat, in an envelope, somewhere dark and cool**, away from heat and plastic sleeves.
- **Keep the restored copy with the original.** Before you throw away a paper receipt, check with your accountant, employer or tax office whether they accept a digital copy.

## Questions about faded receipts

### Can you restore a receipt that has faded completely?

No. If no trace of the print is left on the paper, there is nothing for a photo to pick up. Ask the shop for a copy instead: many can reprint a receipt or find the sale from your card statement.

### Is a photo of a receipt as good as the original?

For many expense claims it is, but rules for tax records differ between countries and employers. Check with your accountant, employer or tax office before you throw the paper copy away.

### Which is better for a faded receipt, grayscale or black and white?

Grayscale usually keeps more of very faint print. Black and white gives a cleaner page that prints and faxes well, but can lose the faintest strokes. Try both and keep the one where every number is readable.

### Why does my receipt photo look worse than the receipt?

Usually because of the light. Indoor light is dim and uneven, so the camera adds noise and the faint print sinks into it. Take the photo near a window in daylight, without the flash.
